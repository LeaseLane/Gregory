    Neighbourhood profile

    Renting an apartment in the Jésuites neighbourhood.

    The Jésuites neighbourhood occupies the part of the borough between Boulevard Henri-Bourassa and Boulevard du Loiret, south of Boulevard Jean-Talon. Boulevard Louis-XIV and Autoroute Félix-Leclerc run through it. Almost entirely residential, it has a few institutional and commercial areas, including Carrefour Charlesbourg, on Boulevard Henri-Bourassa.

    In the 2021 census, the neighbourhood had 15,975 residents, and 42.7% of households were tenants. It is home to the Patro de Charlesbourg, the Arpidrome, a sports complex adjoining the École polyvalente de Charlesbourg, and Parc Guillaume-Mathieu. The Charlesbourg campus of Cégep Limoilou and the École Joseph-Paquin are also located there.

    • Housing

      Single-family houses and apartments in buildings of fewer than five storeys, mostly built after 1960.

    • Nearby

      Église Saint-Charles-Borromée · Parc de la Montagne-des-Roches · Corridor des Cheminots

    • Good to know

      Boulevard Henri-Bourassa connects the neighbourhood to Limoilou; Autoroute Félix-Leclerc runs through it to the south.

    History

    The history of Jésuites.

    The key dates, then the story in four chapters.

    1. 01

      Chapter 01 · 1626 – 1855

      //

      The neighbourhood takes its name from the Jesuits, to whom Henri de Lévis granted the seigneury of Notre-Dame-des-Anges on March 10, 1626. The estate, enlarged in 1637 and 1652, extends north from the Saint-Charles River. The Jesuits did not begin developing it until 1652. The seigneury kept its integrity and agricultural character until 1855.

    2. 02

      Chapter 02 · 1742 – 1854

      //

      In 1742, the Jesuits built a stone water mill, one of a series of six on their seigneury. The Rivière des Commissaires, which flowed 1.5 km to the north, was diverted by hand to feed it, hence the expression “bringing water to the mill”. Until 1854, the miller was only a tenant of the mill, where he lived with his family.

    3. 03

      Chapter 03 · 1854 – 1940

      //

      With the abolition of the seigneurial system in 1854, the mill became private property. It served in turn as a flour mill, a match factory and a blacksmith shop; its last owner, the blacksmith Philippe Lefebvre, stopped milling flour there in 1940. In 1936, the Québec–Charlesbourg road, today’s Boulevard Henri-Bourassa, linked Chemin de la Canardière to the village of Charlesbourg.

    4. 04

      Chapter 04 · 1956 – 2008

      //

      Charlesbourg urbanized in the second half of the 20th century. The parish of Saint-Jérôme was erected in 1956; its church, opened in 1960, was completed in 1967. The City of Charlesbourg acquired the mill in 1982 and restored it in 1990–1992; it now houses a children’s history museum. The neighbourhood’s name was adopted shortly after the election of its first council, on February 19, 2008.
